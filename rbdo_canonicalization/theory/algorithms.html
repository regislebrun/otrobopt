Specialized algorithms¶

The generic RobustOptimizationProblem accepts any robustness or reliability measure, and the canonicalizer reduces it to a deterministic problem over . This is the reference implementation: it is exact, it supports every measure, and it makes no assumption on the reliability structure. Some algorithms exploit a narrower structure and therefore use the dedicated RBDOProblem:

  • a scalar classical Function objective;

  • one JointChanceMeasure constraint;

  • deterministic bounds, inequality constraints and equality constraints.

The class keeps the distribution parameter fixed by default. The canonical problem returned by getCanonicalProblem() freezes it; the overloads that accept CanonicalizationOptions expose the active parameter in the canonical decision vector. The canonical inequality constraint gathers the joint chance index first and the deterministic inequalities afterwards. The measure itself stays available through getCanonicalJointChanceMeasure(), so an algorithm reads the target probability and the comparison operator from the measure instead of inferring them from the index value.

The algorithms below are optional: when the structure is not present, the canonicalizer and a general nonlinear solver remain the fallback. They are added incrementally, and the summary table at the end records the current status.

The generic canonicalizer¶

The canonicalizer solves the problem over with a fixed reference . It is the most general method and the reference for the others. Its strengths are:

  • it applies to every robustness and reliability measure, including quantile, variance, worst case and aggregated measures;

  • it is exact under the invariance theorem, with no reliability approximation;

  • it exposes the distribution parameter as an ordinary decision variable, with bounds and a starting point.

Its limits are:

  • every measure evaluation may contain a quadrature, a quantile solve, a chance probability or an inner optimization, so the cost per iteration is high;

  • the measure gradients are often finite differences, and chance and quantile measures are non-smooth, which degrades gradient-based solvers;

  • it does not exploit the reliability structure, so it cannot reuse a design point or a reliability index between iterations.

Strengths.

Limits.

  • The cost is the product of the outer iterations and the inner reliability solves; each inner solve may require many limit-state evaluations.

  • The scalarized limit state is nonsmooth, and the inner solver may converge to a local design point when the limit state is nonconvex.

  • The EXACT joint-chance mode is a FORM approximation, not an exact system probability. The BONFERRONI mode is conservative.

  • The method applies to a fixed distribution; it does not expose as a decision variable.

  • The distribution parameter is fixed in the current implementation.

Sample-average approximation¶

The sample-average algorithm keeps a calibration sample drawn once at the beginning of the run and replaces the chance constraint by its exact empirical counterpart,

The empirical probability is used without a smooth surrogate, so the constraint is a step function and the outer solver must be derivative-free. Any target is accepted, including targets for which the reliability index is undefined.

Because the empirical probability is piecewise constant, the solver may fail from an infeasible starting point. The algorithm therefore looks for a feasible start along the gradient of the scalarized limit state and along the coordinate directions before it calls the outer solver. The reported solution satisfies the empirical constraint exactly.

After the optimization, the safe probability is estimated again on an independent validation sample and a Wilson 95% confidence interval is reported. The interval measures the sampling error of the validation estimate; it does not measure the bias of the calibration sample or the quality of the outer solver.

Strengths.

  • It is distribution-free: it applies to any measure with finite moments, including mean, variance, quantile, chance and worst case, and it does not require a standard space or a FORM analysis.

  • It handles parameter-dependent distributions naturally, because is part of the canonical decision vector.

  • Common random numbers give a deterministic, reproducible problem and reduce the variance of the comparison between designs.

Limits.

  • The solution depends on the calibration sample; the empirical constraint is a step function and the outer solver may stall or return a design that is feasible only for that sample.

  • The sample size controls the bias and the variance, and the cost grows with and with the dimension of .

  • The validation interval quantifies the sampling error only; a rigorous feasibility guarantee requires the scenario approach or a dedicated chance-constrained method.

  • The method does not exploit the reliability structure and can be expensive when the limit-state function is costly.

Single-loop methods¶

The single-loop method eliminates the inner reliability loop by approximating the most probable point as a function of the design. For FORM, the KKT conditions of the reliability problem give the design point as the solution of a small system that can be updated together with the design. The method then solves one design loop with the updated design point.

Strengths.

  • It is the cheapest of the reliability-based methods: it avoids a full inner reliability solve at every outer iteration.

  • It is accurate for smooth limit states and moderate reliability levels, and it is easy to combine with a gradient-based design solver.

Limits.

  • It relies on a FORM/KKT approximation of the design point, so it is less robust than SORA when the limit state is highly nonlinear or the target probability is extreme.

  • It is naturally formulated for individual chance constraints; the joint event requires an additional treatment.

  • It is planned, not yet implemented.

Scenario and cutting-plane methods¶

The scenario approach draws independent scenarios and enforces the constraints for every scenario. For convex problems, the scenario solution is feasible for the true chance constraint with a confidence that depends on , the number of decision variables and the confidence level. The sample size grows with the dimension and with the inverse of the confidence, which limits the method to moderate dimensions.

Cutting-plane and conditional-value-at-risk (CVaR) approximations replace the joint chance constraint by a convex conservative approximation and add violated scenarios iteratively. They provide a deterministic outer approximation and can be combined with a linear or convex solver.

Strengths.

  • They provide a probabilistic feasibility guarantee for convex problems, without a FORM approximation.

  • They are distribution-free and apply to joint chance constraints directly.

  • They are complementary to SORA: SORA is design-driven, while scenario and cutting-plane methods are sample-driven outer approximations.

Limits.

  • The guarantee requires convexity; for nonconvex problems the scenario solution is only a heuristic.

  • The sample size grows with the dimension and the confidence, and the resulting problem can be large.

  • The CVaR approximation is conservative and changes the feasible set.

  • They are planned, not yet implemented.

Bilevel worst case and alternating parameter optimization¶

A WorstCaseMeasure is an inner optimization problem over the support of or over an explicit optimization domain. A specialized algorithm alternates the outer design optimization with the inner adversarial optimization, or solves the inner problem by duality when the constraint is convex in . The domain policy of Parameter-dependent distributions and canonicalization is part of the algorithm contract: the inner problem must use the explicit optimization domain stored on the measure, not the range of a reference distribution that may be unbounded.

When the distribution parameter is a decision variable and the canonical problem is large, an alternating scheme solves the design subproblem with fixed and the parameter subproblem with fixed. It is easy to implement with existing solvers and can provide a good warm start for the canonicalizer.

Strengths.

  • The worst-case formulation handles adversarial uncertainty and bounded uncertainty sets, which the mean and chance measures do not capture.

  • Alternating optimization scales to large and avoids a single large canonical problem.

Limits.

  • The inner adversarial problem can be nonconvex, and a local inner solver only gives an upper bound on the worst case.

  • Alternating optimization has no global convergence guarantee and can stall at a coordinatewise local minimum.

  • It is a heuristic, not a replacement for the canonicalizer.
